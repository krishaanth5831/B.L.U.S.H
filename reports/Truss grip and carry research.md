# Carry the truss at one fixed tilt

The cut truss turns in the grip mainly because the carry changes how the gripper is tilted relative to gravity. At the cut, the truss already hangs straight down. When the gripper tilts on the way to the crate, gravity pulls the truss back toward hanging. The project's own simulation shows a pull of up to about 0.06 N·m. Flat pads at 4 N cannot resist that, in simulation or, by every estimate in the notes, in real life. The cheapest fix that would also work on real hardware is to stop asking the pads to fight gravity. Keep the gripper's pitch and wrist roll the same from the cut to the release and let only the base (pan), reach and height change. Then cap acceleration so the truss barely swings, and lower it into the crate before opening. Published harvesters do the same: they let the cluster hang, catch it from below or hook it, and none tries to hold it stiff by the stalk. Pad changes come second and should be chosen by bench tests: rubber faces, a longer grip along the stalk with ridges at the ends, a groove, or a hook. By estimate, today's flat pads would need roughly 11–29 N each to hold a truss held sideways, while rubber ridges 34 mm apart along the stalk would need only about 2 N. A plain warning about the target: in the project's simulation, even a diagnostic perfect hold only reached 77.8 %. Stopping the turning closes about half the gap to 95 %. The rest needs work on swing, placing and collisions near the plant.

The failure the project sees is **pivot**.

## The pads lose against gravity once the carry tilts the gripper

Start with the size of the load. In the four project-sim scenes where the truss turned 70–87°, it weighed 53 g. Its centre of mass sat 115 mm from the grip along the stalk. At the cut the stalk pointed almost straight down, so the truss already hung and the load on the pads was small: 0.005–0.02 N·m. The load rose to **0.05–0.065 N·m when the planned path turned the gripper 90–120° against gravity** (project sim). That matches the simple product 0.053 kg × 9.81 × 0.115 m ≈ 0.060 N·m for a truss held sideways. So the load comes from the path, not from the cut.

Now the grip. For a round stalk pressed evenly along a flat pad, the friction that resists pivot is μ·F·L/2 for the two pads together. Here μ is the friction coefficient, F the force per pad and L the length of stalk touching the pad (**estimate**, derived in the grip-mechanics note). Today the stalk crosses the 13 mm side of the pad. Rubber on a plant stalk measured μ = 0.81–1.02 on reed (**real hardware**, [Vronskis & Kakitis 2023](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65142f5d925d9e099bc6f9e2)). With μ = 0.8–1.0, F = 4 N and L = 13 mm, the hold is **about 0.021–0.026 N·m**. Real flat pads did worse than this even-pressure ideal. A sensorised gripper with flat printed pads reached only about 40–80 % of the ideal twisting resistance (**real hardware**, [Waltersson & Karayiannidis](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.19660)). Bare PLA is worse still: hard plastics on reed gave μ = 0.12–0.37. The project sim uses μ = 1.5, which gives about 0.04 N·m. The model's own code marks that value as a guess, and no source supports it for a tomato stalk.

Put the two together. Even by the ideal formula, a real flat rubber pad holds well under half the peak load. The gripper can tilt only about **20–26° away from "truss hanging" by the ideal formula, and about 8–20° once the measured shortfall of real flat pads is applied, before the pad lets go** (**estimate**: sin ψ = hold ÷ 0.060 N·m). That is for the light 53 g sim truss. The one measured commercial cherry truss in the notes weighed **168 ± 16 g with 15 ± 3 fruit** ([preprint, arXiv 2610.18160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18160), **real hardware**). If its centre of mass sat at a similar distance, it would load the pads about three times as much (**estimate**). Turning is therefore not a simulation quirk. The project reached the same verdict with its own estimate: a real flat pad would hold about 0.02–0.04 N·m, "so the real truss would also turn on these paths".

Spin and rock matter less. Spin resistance has only the 2 mm stalk radius as a lever: 2·μ·F·r ≈ 0.013 N·m at μ = 0.8 and 4 N (**estimate**). Spinning until the fruit hang lowest is the natural resting state and is cheaper to allow than to fight. Rocking has no friction term. It is resisted by the pads having to separate, about F·L ≈ 0.05 N·m for 13 mm at 4 N, **but only if the pads are guided to stay parallel** (**estimate**). A pad that sits on one central spring and can tilt loses almost all of that. In the sim each pad rides on a straight slide, so it cannot tilt. Whether the printed v6 pads are guided the same way should be checked on the CAD.

A diagnostic shows how much grip alone is worth. It is not an option, because it cannot be built. When the cut truss was fixed rigidly to the gripper from cut to release, 108 finished scenes reached **77.8 %, against 62 % for today's teacher on the same scenes** (**project sim**). Turning therefore explains about half the gap. With a perfect hold, these failures remained: fruit off on the way to the crate (6), a neighbour bumped at the cut (4), on the way (3) or while retreating (2), and no plan (5). Read 77.8 % as an indicator, not a hard ceiling, because a gravity-aligned carry also changes the paths.

The full picture of today's best variant shows the same thing. It failed 54 of 180 scenes, and 95 % allows only 9. The 54 split as follows: no plan 24, all at the carried-truss check; fruit knocked off or a neighbour pushed on the way to the crate 14; truss not fully in the crate 6; neighbour bumped at the cut 5; damage on the way home 3; other 2 (**project sim**). Tuning release poses is spent. The best of three planner variants per scene reached 73 %, and 48 scenes failed in all three. The 70 % variant beat today's 66.7 %. A sign test puts the chance that a gap this size is luck at about 24 %, so the gain is not proven.

## Real harvesters let the cluster hang, catch it or hook it

No published tomato tool tries to hold a cut truss stiff by its stalk alone. The designs fall into three families, summarised here.

| System | Evidence | How the cut cluster is held | Force | What the paper says happens |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kondo et al. 2010, tomato cluster | real hardware, greenhouse, 20 clusters | spring grasper beside the cutter, cluster hangs | 20 N on an 8 mm stalk; 40 N "necessary… for robot's quick movement" with 400 g clusters | 50 % overall success; 1 of 20 lost a fruit, and the authors ask for control that avoids impacts on the cluster ([J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/eaef/3/1/3_1_20/_pdf)) |
| Ji et al., truss, cited by de Haan | real hardware | two rubber-plated fingers pinch the stalk end | not given | "the grasped truss hangs almost vertically downwards, making accurate placement difficult" ([arXiv 2103.01272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01272)) |
| AHPPEBot 2024 | real hardware, commercial greenhouse | nothing holds the stalk; the truss drops into a mesh pocket under the saw | none | 86.67 % (13/15) autonomous, 89.58 % (43/48) with manual poses ([arXiv 2405.06959](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06959)) |
| Denso patent NL2026342B9 | patent | spring grip with sharp biting ridges, plus a hard stop leaving a gap slightly smaller than the stalk | not given | crushing makes the stalk leak juice, turn slippery and slip; released "immediately above the collection box" ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/NL2026342B9/en)) |
| Kok 2024, hook gripper | real hardware, lab, harvested trusses | hook under the stalk near the centre of mass | weight only | pinch prototypes made trusses "swing and wobble"; the hook gave a stable grip at higher speed ([TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:d81da13c-650f-4b86-be9c-d579cdba7558)) |
| Kondo 1995, grape | real hardware | finger and cutter on one motor, plus a pusher on the bunch | 10 N grasp | the pusher exists "to reduce the swinging of the bunch at the time of carrying, and to orient the bunch at the time of release" ([JARQ PDF](https://www.jircas.go.jp/sites/default/files/publication/jarq/29-3-171-177_0.pdf)) |
| Artemy (Denso + Certhon), Ridder | vendor | Certhon's page confirms crates carried on the robot and swapped all at once; Ridder says its shears cut and "secure" the truss. Trade-press summaries say Artemy's shears grab and cut, but that text was not seen in a source that was read | not published | no gripper details or success rates ([Certhon](https://certhon.com/denso-and-certhon-introduce-artemy-a-fully-automated-cherry-truss-tomato-harvesting-robot/), [Ridder](https://ridder.com/harvesting-robot)) |

Three lessons carry over. First, **hanging is the default, and placement is where hanging hurts.** Ji's team said so directly, and the project's "truss not fully in the crate" failures are the same problem. Second, **forces in the literature are much higher than 4 N, but so are the loads.** Kondo's 40 N served 400 g clusters on 5–12 mm stalks, about 10 N per 100 g. Scaled by weight alone, the 53 g sim truss would need about 5 N and a 168 g commercial truss about 17 N (**estimate**; thinner stalks give less contact, so this flatters small stalks). Raising force is not free, though. In Kok's lever tests, every load tried bruised or marked the peduncle, and thinner stalks fared worse (**real hardware**, [Kok 2024](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:d81da13c-650f-4b86-be9c-d579cdba7558)). Repeated foam-finger grasps abraded peduncle skin, and pinch-held trusses slipped out mid-air (**real hardware**, [van den Bent et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17170v2)). Shape, not squeeze, is the direction every tool that wanted a firm hold took. Third, **the only measured fruit loss in transport came from impacts and jerks.** That points at swing control and wall clearance, not at grip strength.

One close relative deserves a mention. De Haan's team used an Interbotix PincherX 150, a 5-joint hobby arm much like this one, with foam fingers and rubber knobs that enclose the stalk. Even with grasps chosen near the centre of mass, "undesired tilting of the second model was frequently observed" (**real hardware**, plastic fruit, [arXiv 2103.01272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01272)). Small arms with soft pinch fingers tilt the truss. That is the baseline expectation, not a bug.

## Pads should beat the load by shape, not by squeezing harder

At 4 N, only geometry raises the hold enough to survive a tilted carry. The table compares buildable pad options against the 0.060 N·m peak load of the 53 g sim truss. It gives the hold at today's 4 N, and the force per pad each option would need to hold 0.060 N·m with rubber at μ = 0.8. All values are **estimates** from the grip-mechanics note, recomputed with the project's numbers. None has been measured on a stalk.

| Pad option | Hold at 4 N per pad | Force per pad to hold 0.060 N·m (μ 0.8) | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Today: flat, stalk across 13 mm, rubber μ 0.8–1.0 | 0.021–0.026 N·m (real flat pads reach 40–80 % of this) | ≈ 11.5 N ideal, ≈ 14–29 N for real flat pads | more force marks the stalk (see below) |
| Flat, ridges at both ends of the 13 mm side (11 mm apart) | ≈ 0.035–0.044 N·m | ≈ 6.8 N | edge ridges mark the stalk |
| Stalk turned to lie along the 36 mm side, even pressure | ≈ 0.058 N·m at μ 0.8, 0.072 at μ 1.0 | ≈ 4.2 N | marginal: real pads fall short of the ideal; needs ≥ 30 mm of straight stalk between cut and first branch (not measured) |
| Along 36 mm with rubber ridges at both ends (34 mm apart), μ 0.5–1.0 | ≈ 0.068–0.136 N·m | ≈ 2.2 N | same stalk-length need; changes how the jaw approaches |
| V-groove along the stalk on both jaws, 13 mm, pads guided parallel | 60° V: up to 0.090; 90° V: up to 0.052; 120° V: up to 0.030 N·m | 60°: 2.7 N; 90°: 4.6 N; 120°: 8.0 N (upper bounds) | a soft stalk bends and flattens, shrinking all of these; see the trade-off below |
| Hook or fork the stalk rests on, 2 cm from the pinch | the hook carries the torque by shape | ≈ 3 N of contact at the hook; the pads only carry the weight | must not catch neighbours or leaves at the cut |
| Denso-style hard stop, gap a little below stalk diameter, with small biting ridges | not quantified | not quantified | patent gives no numbers; marks the stalk stub |

The V-groove has a built-in trade-off. A narrow 60° V holds by shape at low force. But with rubber faces it can wedge the stalk and keep it stuck when the jaw opens over the crate: it self-locks once μ is above about 0.58 (**estimate**). A wider 90–120° V releases cleanly, but at 4 N it no longer beats the load by shape alone. A V is therefore a good partner for a gravity-aligned carry, not a replacement for one.

What limits force from above? No source gives a crush threshold for a tomato peduncle. In Kok's lever tests, every load tried left bruising or surface marks, worse on thin stalks (**real hardware**). A strawberry study held a peduncle at up to 10 N without crushing it ([arXiv 2207.12552](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12552), **real hardware**, abstract only, a different plant). Kondo used 20–40 N, but on stalks two to three times thicker. Something near 10 N on a 3–5 mm stalk is a plausible ceiling to test against, not a known safe value.

The formulas behind the table are simple. Ridges spaced D apart give μ·F·D instead of μ·F·L/2, roughly double for the same force. A V-groove forces a pivoting stalk to climb the flanks against the springs, about F·L·cot β, where β is half the V angle. A hook turns the gravity torque into a plain contact force. The two-phase gripper of Chavan-Dafle and colleagues used exactly this switch on real hardware. A light point contact lets the part swing under gravity; more force seats it in a V-groove for a firm hold ([US9808936B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9808936); [CASE 2015](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/107782)). Every hardware system in the notes that wanted a fixed grasp after pivoting added a groove. That is a fair sign that friction alone at low force is not a robust hold.

Material advice is short. Never let bare PLA touch the stalk. Use rubber or soft TPU faces. TPU 95A and PLA pads gave μ = 0.36–0.54 on everyday objects, with TPU higher (**real hardware**, [Waltersson & Karayiannidis](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.19660)). Rubber on reed gave 0.81–1.02. Nobody has measured any pad on a tomato stalk, wet or dry. Sap will lower μ, and Kok saw tomato dirt on pads reduce grip ([Kok 2024](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:d81da13c-650f-4b86-be9c-d579cdba7558)). A friction-only grip gets worse over a shift. A groove, ridge stance or hook keeps working when μ drops.

There is one important link to the next section. If the carry keeps the truss hanging, the pads only have to resist acceleration. At a 0.8 m/s² cap that is about 0.053 × 0.8 × 0.115 ≈ 0.005 N·m (**estimate**), well inside even today's flat rubber pads. Pad geometry then becomes insurance for detours, heavier trusses and wet stalks, rather than the main fix. That is why the order of work puts the carry first and the jaw later.

## Plan the carry for five joints: fixed tilt, slow ramps, lower then release

### Keep pitch and roll fixed, and move with pan, reach and height

The arm has pan, shoulder lift, elbow, wrist flex, wrist roll and the jaw. Pan turns about the vertical. Lift, elbow and flex share parallel horizontal axes, so together they set one pitch angle. Gravity as seen from the gripper therefore depends only on that summed pitch and on wrist roll, not on pan (**estimate**, the researchers' own kinematics; check the joint directions in the simulation's robot model file). The project's first test agrees: held this way, the planned change in gripper-versus-gravity was only 0.1–1.2° (**project sim**). Hold pitch and roll at their cut values through every via-pose (the fixed in-between poses the arm passes through) and the release, and the truss feels exactly the load it felt when it was cut. Reach, height and pan stay free. Moving smoothly between two poses with the same pitch keeps the pitch constant all the way, as long as every joint starts and stops together on the same profile. No joint-angle solving is needed along the way. On hardware, servo lag that differs between lift, elbow and flex would break this, so commanded and measured pitch should be compared.

The project has started testing this as the opt-in `V6_ALIGN` switch. In four hand-picked worst scenes, the results were mixed but clear about the mechanism (**project sim**). In 9730003 the stalk stayed at 10–14° instead of 87°, and the scene succeeded. In 9730018 it stayed at 19–21° instead of 85°, and succeeded. In 9730005 a detour used old via-poses that did not keep the tilt; the stalk swung to 55° and back, but the scene still succeeded. In 9730015 a retreat with free orientation lost a fruit, and the scene failed. Where the tilt was held, the turning stopped. Where it was not, the turning came back. Four scenes prove nothing about the 180-scene rate. The next step the project is building, putting via-poses and the retreat on the same pitch and roll and then running the paired 180-scene comparison, is the right test.

Pose selection then becomes a filter. Accept a scene only if one shared pitch and roll reach the cut, every via-pose and at least one release spot. A short list of two to four allowed carry tilts keeps this deterministic (**estimate**). The effect on the 24 "no plan" failures, all at the carried-truss check, is not known yet. With the tilt held, the carried-truss model "as at the cut" becomes the correct one (**project sim**), which should remove false collisions from a truss modelled at a stale angle. But a fixed tilt also removes paths. In scene 9730005 the straight move was blocked and only a tilt-breaking detour remained. The one hint is the diagnostic perfect hold, which had 5 "no plan" failures in 108 scenes. The 180-scene run should report this class separately. Before calling any scene impossible, re-check it with the truss modelled as hanging straight down, plus a small swing margin. A real-robot planner did this for a pinched object. It treated the grip as a free hinge and kept the object hanging straight down at every step, and it reports that the object "behaves like a pendulum" (**real hardware**, [Costanzo et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.11018)). That paper generates its paths with an optimiser. Only the hanging model is borrowed here, as a collision check.

A sixth joint is missing, and this limit should be stated plainly. With pitch and roll fixed, pan sets the direction the pads face. The truss's swing plane therefore turns with the base. On five joints, the swing plane at release is decided by where the crate sits, so it becomes a layout choice. Changing roll to re-aim it would break the gravity alignment. Do that only once the truss rests on the crate floor.

### Ramp slowly enough that the truss barely swings

A hanging truss is a pendulum. With its centre of mass 115 mm below the grip, its swing period is at least 2π√(0.115/9.81) ≈ 0.68 s, so it swings at 1.5 Hz or slower (**estimate**). That is close to what Kondo measured on real hanging clusters of large-fruited tomatoes, much heavier than a cherry truss: **1.53–1.93 Hz, mean 1.70 Hz** (**real hardware**, [Kondo et al. 2009](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/eaef/2/3/2_3_108/_article/-char/en)). Kondo's input shaping, timed to each cluster's frequency, damped the swing. But the paper warns that the benefit "may be lost at about 25% of the error" in the assumed frequency, and that beyond that the swing can grow (**published sim**). Fine-tuned shapers are fragile. By standard shaper theory, the basic zero-vibration shaper tolerates only about ±3 % frequency error, and its three-impulse version about ±14 % (theory as stated in [Baklouti et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.11676), whose hardware tests were on a cable robot). At 30 Hz control ticks, rounding alone exceeds the narrow band.

The robust recipe is cruder and has three parts (all **estimates** unless noted). First, cap the acceleration of the pinch point, not of each joint, at about 0.8 m/s². A steady acceleration a leans a pendulum by tan⁻¹(a/g), here about 5°, and a sudden step can double that. Second, pass every joint command through the same moving average about one swing period long, roughly 0.7 s or 21 ticks. Applying it to every joint keeps the pitch constant. Third, wait one period after the cut and again above the crate before lowering. Watch pan too. Turning the base at 2 rad/s with the truss 0.25 m out adds 1.0 m/s² of sideways acceleration. A 25 cm carry at these limits takes about 1.5 s, which is cheap. For scale, suction-held packages on a real robot "seldom detached" below 8° of peak swing, and swings above 16° went with detachment ([Shukla et al., CASE 2025](https://cdn.amazon.science/b3/58/2f1438b34288a50ec488ef3b3df6/case25-0617-fi.pdf), **real hardware**). These thresholds are for packages on suction cups, not trusses. They show only that bounding swing per move is enough to plan safe transport.

Fruit should survive gentle swing. The weakest measured cherry joint broke at about 0.55 N, with the main pedicel glued so the break happened at the calyx ([Xie et al. 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/11/9/815), **real hardware**). A 5–19 g cherry weighs 0.05–0.19 N, so at rest the margin is 3–10×. Fruit therefore come off through jerks and impacts, not through hanging. That fits Kondo's one lost fruit and the 14 "fruit off or neighbour pushed" failures in the project sim.

### Put the crate where the carry is mostly pan, and lower before opening

Commercial robots keep the crate close to the arm: Ridder and Artemy load crates on board (**vendor**). On the bench, the matching move is a layout change (**estimate**). Put the crate's drop spot at the same distance from the pan axis as the typical cut. Raise it on a stand so "above the crate" sits at the same height as "after the retreat". Turn its long side along the pan arc. The carry then reduces to a short straight retreat, a pan and a short vertical lowering, with only pan moving through a large angle. Changing the layout changes the 180-scene benchmark, so compare old and new teachers on the same layout.

Placing a hanging truss in a 5 cm crate is **not yet tested** in the project sim. No paper studies lowering, laying or dropping a cluster into a container. The proposed release is the researchers' design. Stop above the crate at the carry tilt, with the lowest fruit clear of the rim by the wall height plus a swing margin. Wait one period. Lower straight down slowly, at about 0.1 m/s or less, until the lowest fruit is about 1 cm above the floor or the trusses already there. Optionally keep lowering 2–4 cm while sliding 2–4 cm toward the empty side, so the truss folds onto its side instead of standing on its bottom fruit. Then open slowly and lift straight up at the same tilt. Releasing a truss that stands upright on its lowest fruit lets it topple, and the top fruit then falls almost the full truss length.

Drop limits come from larger tomatoes only. Single drops of 10 cm bruised 15–73 % of breaker-stage fruit internally ([J. Amer. Soc. Hort. Sci. 1992](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df8e4db766d82b180260d0), **real hardware**, standard-size fruit, abstract only). A layer of foam over a hard surface "greatly reduced bruising" ([USDA MRR 513](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/313075/files/mrr513.pdf), not read in full). No cherry-tomato threshold exists. Keeping any free fall under about 2–3 cm and lining the crate floor with foam are sensible margins, but they are guesses. Industry stores trusses stacked peduncle-up in crates ([van den Bent et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.17170v2)), so slow truss-on-truss contact is normal.

## The simulator both flatters the grip and punishes it

The project sim's grip errs in two opposite directions. That is why turning in simulation does not, on its own, prove or disprove anything about the real jaw.

It **flatters** the grip in two ways. First, a round stalk on a box pad gets exactly two contact points per pad, at the two ends of the overlap ([MuJoCo docs, pair-wise colliders](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html#pair-wise-colliders); seen in the **project sim**, which logged 4 contacts, 2 per pad, at the ends of the 13 mm overlap). Two end points resist pivot twice as well as an evenly pressed line (**estimate**). Second, the sliding friction of 1.5 is marked as a guess in the model's code (`sim/scene.py`, `PAD_FRICTION`). Rubber on a stalk is nearer 0.8–1.0.

The twisting coefficient needs a short note, because the test rig and the project differ here. MuJoCo combines two surfaces' friction values by taking the larger of each, unless one surface has priority ([MuJoCo modeling docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/modeling.html)). On the test rig, with no priority, the stalk's 0.01 m overrode the pad's 0.002 m. In the project's model the pads do carry priority (`_finger_contact` in `sim/scene.py`), so the pad's 0.002 m governs. That value is also a guess. The code records that it was lowered from 0.02 m on 2026-11-03, because at 0.02 the pads held a truss sticking stiffly out sideways, which real pads would not. The docs disagree on whether this number is a contact-patch diameter or a radius, which matters by a factor of two.

It **punishes** the grip through creep. MuJoCo's friction "does not guarantee an exact zero-velocity stick state… a persistent tangential load can produce a nonzero steady slip velocity. This is expected behavior" ([MuJoCo docs, preventing slip](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/modeling.html#preventing-slip)). A maintainer called a pinched capsule creeping out of a grip "WAI", working as intended ([issue #3328](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco/issues/3328)). On the test rig, a 4 mm stalk across the 13 mm face crept about 4°/s about the pad normal at only 0.02 N·m. Spin crept far faster, about 44°/s at 0.01 N·m (**test-rig sim**, 0.09 kg stalk body; in the pivot test a 0.01 kg body crept about twice as fast, so the project's rates will differ). Two results there run against intuition. Creep did not depend on clamp force, and lower friction crept less. "More friction" is therefore not a creep fix in MuJoCo. The rig's rates do not transfer directly. The project's pads use a stiffer contact setting (solimp 0.95 against the rig's 0.9), which MuJoCo's docs say roughly halves creep.

The project sim has separated these two effects. With MuJoCo's no-slip pass switched on (5 iterations) and nothing else changed, the stalk stayed within about 1–6° for most of the carry in three of the four bad scenes, and about 12° in the fourth. Every scene still slipped 20–80° later, once the load reached about 0.07 N·m (**project sim**). The slow drift was creep, an artefact. The late jump was a real friction release. One gap is unexplained. The project computed 0.09–0.10 N·m of capacity, and the test rig held 0.10 N·m with no-slip on, yet the project scenes let go near 0.07 N·m. Moving loads, the weight shared with the twisting load, or slightly non-parallel pads could explain the difference, but nobody has checked.

Outside checks are thin. The most direct sim-to-real comparison found MuJoCo predicting about 0.6 rad of pivot where a real gripper pivoted about 1 rad ([arXiv 1702.07252](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.07252), **real hardware vs sim**, read through a summary). No paper has fitted MuJoCo's twisting coefficient to measured pivot torques. The established modelling practice is several primitive boxes per pad, with the pad given priority and its own contact settings, as in Menagerie's Robotiq 2F-85: "Broke up collision pads into two pads for more contacts" ([Menagerie README](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_menagerie/blob/main/robotiq_2f85/README.md)).

These solver settings are not fixes for the teacher. No-slip, a higher impratio (a solver setting that stiffens friction relative to the push between surfaces), splitting the pad into several boxes and setting the twisting coefficient change how honestly the sim reports a grip. Used to make the grip hold, they would amount to the ruled-out idealised hold by another route. Pair them with bench-measured friction. A more honest sim pulls both ways: less creep, but also a weaker real-friction hold. The corrected score is therefore unknown until it is run. The practical rule is to score every teacher change twice, once with today's settings and once with no-slip on and μ near 0.8. Then keep only changes that win in both. No-slip runs can blow up numerically; the test rig diverged at loads near the spin limit. Flag those runs and leave them out of the count rather than scoring them as failures.

## Nine numbers are still unmeasured, and a kitchen table can measure them

None of the tests below has been run. They need a kitchen scale, calipers, a phone, cups of water and printed jigs. Most are proposals from the researchers, building on standard methods: tilted-plane friction ([Jahanbakhshi et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6475739/)) and slow pull tests ([Xie et al. 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/11/9/815)). Use freshly cut trusses of the real cultivar, about 5 stalks × 5 repeats per condition.

| Unmeasured number | Why it matters | Simple test |
|---|---|---|
| Pivot and spin breakaway torque, real pads at real force | Decides whether real pads turn, and calibrates both sim friction values. **The key test** | Clamp a stalk in the real jaw or a printed copy with the real springs. Tape a skewer lever to the stalk and hang a cup 40–80 mm out. Add water in 5 g steps and film against a printed protractor. Torque = m·g·d. Also hold at 50 % and 80 % for 30 s to see whether real pads creep (expected: no) |
| Stalk-on-pad friction μ, dry and sap-wet, per pad material | Sets every hold value above; the sim's 1.5 is unsupported | Glue pad material to a board, put 3 stalk pieces under a loaded sled, tilt until it slides: μ = tan α |
| Pad indentation and contact width | Sets the sim's twisting coefficient and pad springs | Press a marker-coloured stalk onto paper over the pad with 1–4 N and measure the print width; measure sink depth with calipers |
| Truss mass and centre of mass | Sets the gravity load and the swing period | Weigh it. Hang it from the grip point in two orientations, photograph against a plumb line and intersect the lines |
| Straight stalk length between cut and first branch | Decides whether the 36 mm stance option is possible (needs ≥ 30 mm) | Calipers on 20 trusses |
| Actual pad force, at preload and at full travel | 4 N is a design value | Push each pad onto the kitchen scale with a printed pusher |
| Fruit pull-off at the knuckle, by ripeness | The 0.58–2.46 N in the sim is a calyx-joint value with the knuckle glued | Hold the truss in the clamp, loop thread round one fruit, add water until it drops; note where it broke |
| Swing frequency and damping of a held truss | Sets the smoothing window and dwell times | Hold the truss in the jaw, nudge it, film at 60 fps and count swings and decay. Kondo measured the same thing with an accelerometer ([2009](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/eaef/2/3/2_3_108/_article/-char/en)) |
| Stalk crush and cut force | Upper limit on clamp force; blade sizing | Squeeze between pad samples on the scale and inspect after 1 h and 24 h; press the real blade through on the scale |

Before printing any new jaw, run the breakaway, friction, truss mass and centre-of-mass, stalk-length and pad-force tests. The breakaway test alone decides whether a flat rubber pad plus a gravity-aligned carry is enough, or whether ridges, a groove or a hook are needed. One more check belongs on the real arm once the carry exists: log commanded and measured pitch during a carry. Gravity alignment only works if the servos track together.

Two cautions apply to the whole plan. First, 95 % is high by real-world standards. The best tomato-truss harvesters in the notes reached about 87–90 % on real hardware, and kiwifruit researchers put economic break-even at 95–97 % ([Waikato](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/d5f2cfe0-097d-422c-8ecf-ce4af49b6d43), not read in full). Second, keep the 48 always-failing scenes visible. Data generators that keep only successes bias the dataset toward easy scenes. MESA measured up to about 15 % deviation per region ([arXiv 2611.05567](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05567), **published sim**). A trained policy can still beat its demonstrator; MimicGen went from 8.2 % generation success to a 76 % agent ([arXiv 2310.17596](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.17596)). It can only do so on scenes its data covers.

## Conclusion

The question changes once the numbers are side by side. "How do we grip harder?" becomes "why are we tilting the truss at all?" The arm's geometry happens to make gravity alignment almost free: pan never changes the load, and pitch plus roll can be held through fixed via-poses. Published harvesters reached the same answer from the other side. They let the cluster hang, catch it from below or hook it, and spend their effort on gentle motion and placement. On this plan, pad design stops being the main fix and becomes a margin against detours, heavier commercial trusses and wet stalks. Its size should come from one afternoon of bench tests, not from friction values chosen in the simulator.

The deeper point is about what 95 % will take. Even a perfect grip leaves about a fifth of scenes failing in the project's simulation, through swing, collisions near the plant and placing. So the teacher's path to 95 % runs through three things: the carry tilt, the swing limits and the release procedure, measured in a simulation made honest in both directions. A stronger jaw alone cannot get there. The jaw comes last, sized by bench numbers.
